Pilates, Menopause and Your Bones

Nina Manova · September 2026 · 5 min read

Menopause changes more than your cycle. In the five to seven years after it, a woman can lose up to 20% of her bone density, according to the Bone Health & Osteoporosis Foundation. It happens quietly. There is no pain and no warning, and for many women the first sign is a fracture.

About one in two women over 50 will break a bone because of osteoporosis. That number is not destiny. Bone is living tissue, and it responds to the loads you put through it at any age.

Why bone changes in midlife

Your skeleton is constantly being rebuilt: old bone is broken down and new bone is laid in its place. Estrogen helps keep that process in balance. As estrogen falls through perimenopause and menopause, breakdown starts to outpace rebuilding, and the spine and hips, where fractures matter most, are especially affected.

Bone also needs a reason to stay strong. The signal is mechanical: muscles pulling on bone, and the body working against gravity and resistance. Take that signal away and bone thins. Give it back, in the right dose and progressed over time, and bone can hold its ground.

What the research says about Pilates

A 2021 meta-analysis of 11 studies of Pilates and yoga in women aged 45 to 78 found that bone density was maintained over the training period, with a small improvement from before to after. Compared with control groups, the difference was not statistically significant. The authors made an important point: at an age when bone loss is expected, holding steady is itself a result. They also noted that most programs ran for only 12 to 32 weeks, which is short for something as slow to change as bone.

The most complete picture so far arrived in 2026. A network meta-analysis in the Journal of Sport and Health Science pooled 74 randomized trials and 5,331 postmenopausal women and compared every major type of exercise. The best results did not come from a single method. They came from a combination: mind-body exercise such as Pilates, paired with resistance training. That pairing produced the broadest benefits, at the lumbar spine, the hip and the whole body. Programs lasting a year or longer produced the most consistent gains.

Put simply: Pilates is good for bone, Pilates with progressive resistance is better, and a year of consistency is what makes it work.

Strength is the missing ingredient

For years, women with low bone density were told to be careful: light weights and gentle movement. The LIFTMOR trial challenged that. Postmenopausal women with osteopenia or osteoporosis trained twice a week for eight months with supervised heavy lifting and impact work. Their lumbar spine density rose by 2.9% while the control group lost 1.2%. At the hip, the trained group held steady while the controls lost 1.9%. Across the whole program there was one minor back strain and no fractures.

The lesson is not that everyone should start deadlifting tomorrow. It is that bone responds to meaningful load, progressed carefully and taught well. The classical Pilates apparatus is built for exactly that. Springs provide resistance that can be increased week by week, and the reformer, chair and Cadillac load the legs, hips, back and arms from many directions.

Balance: the other half of fracture prevention

A fracture usually needs two things: a fragile bone and a fall. Pilates addresses the second directly. A 2021 meta-analysis of 12 trials in 702 older adults found that Pilates improved balance, mobility and functional reach, and reduced fear of falling. The authors were careful to say that high-quality evidence on falls themselves is still limited, but the risk factors moved in the right direction.

Menopause brings other changes too. In a small 2025 randomized trial, eight weeks of Pilates twice a week reduced menopausal symptom scores, including hot flashes, mood and urogenital symptoms, compared with education alone. It is one small study, but it points the same way as the larger picture.

A word of caution about forward bending

Not every exercise suits every spine. In a well-known study of postmenopausal women with spinal osteoporosis, 89% of those given flexion exercises (forward curling, like sit-ups) had new vertebral fractures during follow-up, compared with 16% of those given back-extension exercises. The study was small and is decades old, but its message still shapes clinical practice: with low bone density, repeated loaded forward bending of the spine is best avoided, while strengthening the back extensors is protective.

The traditional Pilates repertoire includes plenty of flexion: the roll-up, the hundred, rolling like a ball. A knowledgeable teacher adapts. For clients with osteopenia or osteoporosis, sessions shift toward spinal extension, hip and leg strength, standing work and balance, with flexion modified or removed. This is where a private, personalized program matters most.

What a bone-smart practice looks like

  • Know your numbers. Ask your doctor whether a DXA bone density scan makes sense for you. Your results shape what is appropriate.
  • Load progressively. Springs, body weight and standing work, increased gradually over months.
  • Favor the back and hips. Strong back extensors, glutes and legs protect the spine and the hip.
  • Train balance on purpose. Single-leg work, standing exercises and controlled transitions.
  • Think in years, not weeks. The best results in the research came from programs of 12 months or more, two to three times a week.

Midlife is not the time to do less. It is the time to train more intelligently. If you are in perimenopause or beyond and want a program built around your bones, bring your questions, and your bone density results if you have them, to a free consultation.

This article is general information, not medical advice. If you have osteoporosis, a previous fracture or another medical condition, check with your doctor before starting a new exercise program.
